\begin{abstract}

ESP32s are low-cost, low-energy microcontrollers with integrated bluetooth and WiFi, and have recently lowered the barrier for entry into prototyping smart devices. ESP32s equipped with capacitive moisture sensors have been suggested for low-cost smart plant watering systems. However, measuring moisture in soil is complex, potentially destructive, and requires careful calibration in order to characterise the response curve mapping soil water content to sensor measurements, which many users are unlikely to undertake. Even accurately measuring soil moisture content requires effort, with variability across soils and environmental conditions, and consumer-grade probes tending to offer coarse measurements only. In order for these low-cost smart sensor systems to be practically useful for this category of user, calibration must be easy, and uncertainty measures available. The objective of this work was to develop a near-automous, free and open source software (FOSS) system for monitoring, with continuously updating calibration and measures of uncertainty. \hl{With our method we jointly estimate scale and shape; using a prior calibration curve we can only achieve FC estimation (shape)} \hl{Could be of interest for scientific operations or others requiring low cost}. \hl{Each sensor has its own offsets/bias due to lolocal soil factors; our method allows calibration for each of them. It's not CONSUMER exactly, it's a tech that consumers or engineers could use or build up elsewhere}. \hl{The ESP32s maybe just a test system for the algorithm}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recent work has proposed the use of ESP32-class microcontrollers and low-cost moisture sensors for automated watering systems and measurement of soil water content (SWC) \cite{biswas2018-solar-pump, purnomo2021-lora, sudarmaji2024-smart-soil, afzal2025-arima}, motivated by mitigating water shortages \cite{afzal2025-arima}, with increased interest in application of IoT sensors to agriculture \cite{ruiz2025-iot-mapping}. As global warming continuous, both flooding and water shortages are increasing world-wide \cite{ficklin2022hydrological}, including in the United Kingdom \cite{lane2021climate}. In previous work, ESP32/capacitive sensor calibration has been performed by gravimetry \cite{sudarmaji2024-smart-soil} and WiFi connections. However, it is expected that calibrated response curves depend on a range of factors, including soil type (i.e. clay content), and temperature \cite{merlin2007-calibration}. Furthermore, end users may find it difficult to undertake calibration for each specific application type. 

Typically, gravimetric sampling can be used to characterise SWC, but is destructive and time consuming; this process often involves drying soil by heating and therefore cannot be applied to existing plant systems. Other methods include time domain reflectometry and capacitance and impedence sensors \hl{cite}. Capacitive moisture sensors, as with all electromagnetic methods (EM) measure SWC indirectly through the dielectric permittivity \cite{kelleners2005-frequency-dependence}, since the dielectric permitivity of water exceeds that of other soil components \hl{cite}, though it is dependent on measurement electrical frequency \cite{kelleners2005-frequency-dependence,sheng2025-frequency} and temperature \cite{jones2005-em-methodology}. Field capacity (FC), the water content at which internal drainage stops, is often utilized \cite{jury2004soil}. 

Ideal ranges for SWC varies across plant species and growth stages. For example, tomatoes (\textit{Lycopersicon esculentum}) may grow best at 70\% field capacity (FC) \cite{liu2019tomato}, while the cactus \textit{Opuntia ficus-indica} was shown to exhibit stress under 30\% FC \cite{lahbouki2023effects}.\\

Existing work has been undertaken on moisture sensor calibration and characterisation of variability. Bogena et al (2017) \cite{bogena2017-calibration} developed a procedure for calibration of low-cost SMT1000 moisture sensors and undertook systematic calibration of hundreds of sensors in a range of media, observing sensor-to-sensor variability and both density and temperature dependence. In contrast, Kizito et al. (2008) \cite{kizito2008-frequency} found that for research-grade ECH2O probes (Decagon Devices Inc., Pullman, WA) and a measurement frequency of 70 MHz, a single calibration curve was sufficient for relating electrical conductivity to SWC across different soil salinities, though they did find variability with the degree of probe immersion and dependency of measurements on temperature, and the sensors used are more an order of magnitude more costly than the ``capacitive soil moisture sensor v.2.0'' used in other studies (e.g. \cite{sudarmaji2024-smart-soil}). 

The objective of this work was to introduce an automated system and statistical method for automated calibration of sensors with minimal user input. This aim was primarily motivated by the challenge end users may find with gravimetric calibration, as well as lack of generalisability of a given calibration curve. We aim to present a method providing automated measurements, calibration, and dashboards for visualisation and alerts.

\subsection{Segmentation}

A hierarchical approach was taken to segmentation with coarse and fine steps. For the target system, three types of processes were defined at different time-scales: shocks caused by watering from above; smooth changes induced by watering from below; loss of moisture due to drying or uptake by a plant. All of these processes cause a response in sensor readings over a period of time. Given that the timescales of watering dynamics are much smaller than those of drying and uptake, we first defined coarse `high-velocity' and `low-velocity' states, with the latter approximately corresponding to equilibrium when drying and uptake is slow. Firstly, irregularly spaced sensor data was smoothed with an exponential moving average \cite{genccay2001introduction} (or exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) \cite{zumbach2001operators}), a single-pole low-pass filter \cite{smith1997scientist} with time-weighted coefficients:

\[
\tilde{X}_t = \exp^{-\Delta t/\tau} \tilde{X}_{i-1} + (1-\exp^{-\Delta t/\tau}) X_i
\]

After smoothing, the data was resampled and derivatives were approximated by finite differences (second order) \cite{griffiths2006numerical}. Two thresholds were defined for identification of high-velocity state regions: a trigger threshold $v_{trigger}$ and a release threshold $v_{release}$ (see relay with hysteresis \cite{khalil2002nonlinear}). Once the trigger is passed at a high instantaneous velocity, it must decrease beyond the release to return to the `low-velocity` state. Given that sensor data decreases with moisture content, only negative velocity regions were taken. Given that the sensor response curve has variable sensitivity to input, sensor values were used for adaptive filtering, with thresholds decreasing with sensor values:

\[
a_{trigger,j} = \frac{a_{high} - a_{low}}{x_{max}-x_{min}}(X_j-x_{min}) + a_{low}
\]

For extracted regions identified as `high-velocity', a log-normal was fit to the data by least squares and normalized root mean square error (RMSE) captured for downstream classification. Matched filtering \cite{smith1997scientist} is common for identifying signals. Log-normal fits were used for simplicity and flexibility. \hl{See theoretical justifications; hydraulic conductivity, multi-paths and Gibrat's law, Richards equation, find citations}.\\

\subsection{Calibration}

\subsubsection{Integral data and the inverse response function}

Let $Y_t$ be the absolute SWC at time $t$, $X_t = h(Y_t)$ the sensor response. We cannot directly measure $Y_t$ non-destructively for an arbitrary container pot. If the total wet soil mass or volume, excluding plant matter, was known, $Y_t$ could be deduced from a relative measurement, combined with a known field capacity of the soil, from a known small volume. These paramters may not be available for an arbitrary container. In the opposite direction, the sensor measurements $X_t$ are readily available, and if the inverse response function $h^{-1}$ is known, $Y_t = h^{-1}(X_t)$ could be computed. Unfortunately, the response function can depend on both soil parameters, environmental factors, and sensor paramters including placement (e.g. how densely packed soil is around the sensor). \\

Another quantity that is readily available is the applied water dosage given to a plant, $\Delta Y_i$, if it is recorded. Paired with sensor measurements $X_i$, in principle the response curve can be reconstructed by considering the integral or "cord" data $\Delta Y_i = Y_{after} - Y_{before} = X_{after} - X_{before}$. For example, if the inverse response was a linear function, assuming no noise, the slope could be reconstructed from $\Delta Y_i$ and $\Delta X_i$, and the intercept from a single measurement $X = h^{-1}(0)$. In practice, the response curve is non-linear, and plants are usually sensitive to water content, tolerating only a narrow range of saturation. As such, only a narrow section of the curve $h^{-1}$ can be sampled. \\

In order to resolve these issues, cord data can be used to estimate absolute soil water content provided a sufficiently flexible parametric form. The sensor response has two plateaus, one at dry (low SWC) and one at wet (high SWC), which can be modelled with polynomials \hl{cite}. For a plant that does not tolerate dry conditions, it is not possible to characterise the dry part of this curve. In our case, this part of the curve was also not of interest, so a simpler function such as an exponential can be used with unknown scale and shape. Given that the response function becomes flat at saturation, the inverse response function becomes vertical at full saturation. In order to model this, the sensor value at full saturation $x_{wet}$ and zero saturation $x_{dry}$ is also a required parameter. In our framework, $x_{wet}$ is a free parameter, and $x_{dry}$ is assumed to be known. It is assumed that $x_{wet}$ is not measurable in general since sampling sufficient soil may be destructive, and $x_{wet}$ depends on sensor packing or placement in heterogeneous media.

\begin{equation}
h^{-1}(X_t) = S \cdot g(X_t) = S \cdot \Big((1-q_{dry})\exp\Big\{-k (1 - u(X_t; x_{wet}, x_{dry})\Big\} + q_{dry}\Big)
\end{equation}

Where $u(X_t)$ is a rescaling of $[x_{wet}, x_{dry}]$ to the interval $[0, 1]$. In principle, any function representing proportional SWC could be used for $g(X_t)$, the shape of the inverse response, and may perform better than an exponential curve. In order to accommodate an intercept at the dry end of the response function, the parameter $q_{dry}$ allows the normalized curve $g(x)$ to be squashed into the interval $[q_{dry}, 1.0]$ to accommodate the non-zero SWC in this part of the curve until the dry end. \\

\subsubsection{Bayesian estimation}

To combine the cord data $(\Delta Y_i, \Delta X_i, X_i)$, dry anchor sample $X = h^{-1}(Y=0)$, prior assumptions on the parametric form of $h^{-1}$, optional prior calibration points, and the unknown scale parameter $S$, we adopt a Bayesian framework. We assume lognormal dosage samples:

\begin{equation}
\log(\Delta Y_i) \sim Normal(\log (h^{-1}(X_{end}; S, k, x_{wet}) - h^{-1}(X_{start}; S, k), \sigma^2)
\end{equation}

With the target parameters $\theta = (S, k, x_{wet}, \sigma^2)$. Priors on the lower bound $x_{wet}$ are taken as uniform on a user-specified interval. Priors on scale $S$ can be estimated from soil volume or total plant weight.

For a given posterior calibration curve sample and input time interval, water consumption rate is approximated by a linear estimate. Current water consumption is calculated by computing the linear rate over a time period for each posterior sample of calibration curves, providing uncertainty over calibration curves.\\

\subsubsection{Combining sensor estimates}

Each sensor has its own local saturation $g_j(x)$, determined by physical heterogeneity. At the same time, the total water content of the container is a global property of the system. Our method estimates, per sensor, the total water content. Given $N$ sensors with voltage measurement vector $\mathbf{x}$. Each inverse sensor response is itself a random variable. We refine our single-sensor model as:

\begin{equation}
SWC(\mathbf{x}) = S \cdot E[g(\mathbf{x})] \approx S \Big(\sum_{j=0}^N g_j(x_j) \Big)
\end{equation}

allowing $S$, a global quantity to be estimated jointly, with separate estimates for the local relative saturation functions $g$ per-sensor.
