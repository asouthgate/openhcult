\begin{abstract}

ESP32s equipped with capacitive moisture sensors have recently been suggested for IoT plant watering systems. However, measuring moisture in soil is complex, potentially destructive, and requires careful calibration in order to characterise the response curve mapping soil water content to sensor measurements, which end consumers are unlikely to undertake. Even accurately measuring soil moisture content requires effort, with variability across soils and environmental conditions, and consumer-grade probes tending to offer coarse measurements only. As a result, in a consumer growing context, soil moisture is commonly ascertained by intuition. If IoT sensor systems are to be practically useful for this category of user, calibration must be easy. The objective of this work was to develop an open source, low-effort, near-automous software system for online calibration and monitoring, with assessment of statistical methods through simulation and real data observed over TODO normal operational hours.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recent work has proposed the use of ESP32-class microcontrollers and low-cost moisture sensors for automated watering systems and measurement of soil water content (SWC) \cite{biswas2018-solar-pump, purnomo2021-lora, sudarmaji2024-smart-soil, afzal2025-arima}, motivated by mitigating water shortages \cite{afzal2025-arima}. As global heating increases, both flooding and increases in water shortages are expected world-wide \hl{cite}. In previous work, ESP32/capacitive sensor calibration has been performed by gravimetry \cite{sudarmaji2024-smart-soil} and WiFi connections. However, it is expected that these curves depend on a range of factors, including soil type (i.e. clay content), temperature \cite{merlin2007-calibration}, and salinity \hl{cite}, with unknown generalisability \hl{cite}. Furthermore, end users may find it difficult to undertake calibration for each specific application type. Overall, there is increasing interest in IoT sensors and their application to agriculture (see \cite{ruiz2025-iot-mapping} for a bibliometric analysis). 

Typically, gravimetric sampling can be used to characterise SWC, which is destructive and time consuming. Other methods include time domain reflectometry and capacitance and impedence sensors \hl{cite}. Capacitive moisture sensors, as with all electromagnetic methods (EM) measure SWC indirectly through the dielectric permittivity \cite{kelleners2005-frequency-dependence}, since the dielectric permitivity of water exceeds that of other soil components \hl{cite}, though it is dependent on measurement electrical frequency \cite{kelleners2005-frequency-dependence,sheng2025-frequency} and temperature \cite{jones2005-em-methodology}.

Bogena et al (2017) \cite{bogena2017-calibration} developed a procedure for calibration of low-cost SMT1000 moisture sensors and undertook systematic calibration of hundreds of sensors in a range of media, observing sensor-to-sensor variability and both density and temperature dependence. In contrast, Kizito et al. (2008) \cite{kizito2008-frequency} found that for research-grade ECH2O probes (Decagon Devices Inc., Pullman, WA) and a measurement frequency of 70 MHz, a single calibration curve was sufficient for relating electrical conductivity to SWC across different soil salinities, though they did find variability with the degree of probe immersion and dependency of measurements on temperature, and the sensors used are more an order of magnitude more costly than the "capacitive soil moisture sensor v.2.0" used in other studies (e.g. \cite{sudarmaji2024-smart-soil}). 

The objective of this work was to introduce an automated system and statistical method for automated calibration of sensors with minimal user input. This aim was primarily motivated by the challenge end users may find with gravimetric calibration, as well as lack of generalisability of a given calibration curve. We aim to present a method providing automated measurements, calibration, and dashboards for visualisation and alerts.

\subsection{Watering event detection}

Raw sensor data is continuously streamed into the database system. From there, event detection is performed on recent time intervals. Event detection is performed in several steps: initial triggers based on outlier time series deltas; stable event detection by testing for stability of change in a window; subsetting to events with a sign consistent with watering; consensus amongst sensors for a single microcontroller; manual exclusion of known non-watering events such as sensor removal or repositioning; evaluation. Firstly, denoting measurement at $t_i$ of sensor $s$ as $X_{i,s}$, which are assumed to be continuously generated by the software system previously described, the distribution of $\Delta X_{i,s}$ in manually-defined event-free time intervals can be gathered, and with a threshold $p$, extreme values are taken as candidates if $|\Delta X_{i,s}|$ is greater than a threshold. In this case, using the median absolute deviation and compute modified Z-scores \cite{iglewicz1993volume}:

\[
M_{i,s} = \frac{0.6745 \cdot (|\Delta X_{i,s}| - median_{w}{|\Delta X_{\cdot,s}|})}{MAD_w}
\]

Given a integer lag $L$ to compute differences, a median window $w$, and a significance threshold $p$, an event was defined at a time point $t_i$ if $p_j > p$ for all $j$ between $i$ and $i+L$. The requirement for a sequence of significant results was used as a simple way to only take stable changes. These were selected manually since obtaining sufficiently good event detection itself was trivial on account of low individual-sensor noise (see results, between-sensor was much larger), although these parameters could be optimised in future work, it is unnecessary for this sensor class. 

\subsection{Noise characterisation}

Sensors are subject to a range of noise sources. We consider two sources of noise: sensor noise per sample $\epsilon_{t}$, which could vary per sensor, per-sensor bias $\epsilon_s$, and per-placement bias $\epsilon_p$, which accounts for variation such as due to variable packing, soil-moisture spatial heterogeneity, or insertion depth. The first source of noise was characterised by retrieving data corresponding to event-free time intervals (possibly at equilibrium). In order to characterise the second and third, sensors were placed in a common pot and regularly removed and reinserted; inherent sensor bias should be stable between these exchange events, and bias due to variable packing should not be.

\subsection{Automated response curve calibration}

Consumer-grade soil moisture probes generally report coarse, normalized measurements. Here, we define soil water content as $Z$ as total water volume in a given container with fixed soil content (such as in a pot), instead of the usual $\theta$, which is the ratio of water to total soil volume. We assume that $Z$ itself is not directly measurable, although at a given watering, $\Delta Z$ is known, unlike $\Delta \theta$. We fix this for a given pot and soil volume, and refer to it as $W$. This is a simplifying assumption that disregards runoff and user measurement error.\\

Many consumer-grade soil moisture probes report a normalized score (e.g. between 0 and 10). Importantly, with only $\Delta Z$ as a water volume, with $Z_{max}$ a normalized score can be obtained. $Z_{max}$ requires experiment to determine. Alternatively, a practical way to determine $X(Z_{max})$ is to take the minimum database sensor value under the assumption that fully soil has occurred at least once. \\

For convenience, the system relies on automatically detected watering events previously described. For a given event at time $t_i$, and sensor $s$, we extract associated $X_{i,s}$. This leads to sampled pairs $(\Delta Z_{i,s}, X_{i,s})$. We asssume the relationship:

\[
X_{i,s} = h(Z_{i,s}) + \epsilon_i + \epsilon_s
\]

Where $\epsilon_i$ is Gaussian sensor measurement noise and a $\epsilon_s$ Gaussian noise offset per-sensor per-placement (accounting for factors such as sensor-specific offsets and variable depth and packing). In this equation, both the response function $h(\cdot)$ and the water content $Z$ are unknown. For simplicity, we assume $h$ is monotonically decreasing and refer to $g=h^{-1}$. In terms of the data, we have samples:

\[
\Big(\frac{\Delta Z}{\Delta X}\Big)_{i,s} \approx \Big(\frac{dz}{dx}\Big)_{i,s} + \epsilon_i + \epsilon_s
\]

where:

\[
\Big(\frac{dZ}{dX}\Big)_{i,s} = g'(X_{i,s})
\]

This is a basic regression problem except that it is required to estimate a derivative $g'$ instead of $g$ itself, and invert to get $h$. We used Gaussian process regression \cite{williams2006gaussian} using Scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011scikit} to estimate $g'$. Then, we sample from $\hat{g'}$ and integrate to produce estimates and CIs for $\hat{g}$.

\[
g'(x) \sim \mathcal{GP}(X, k(X, X'))
\]

For the kernel, a combination of Scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011scikit} kernels \texttt{C(1.0) * RBF(length\_scale=10.0, length\_scale\_bounds=(0.1, 20.0)) + WhiteKernel(noise\_level=0.01, noise\_level\_bounds=(1e-5, 20.0))} was utilized. For integration, \texttt{scipy.integrate.cumulative\_trapezoid} \cite{virtanen2020scipy} was used. In practice, $X(Z_{max})$ is easiest endpoint to obtain because it is the value of $X$ at which all added water is lost as runoff. Alternatively, $X(Z_{min} = 0.0)$ could be obtained as the sensor value of fully dry soil, but at low water content, measured permittivity is instead driven by other factors. Neither is exact but $X(Z_{max})$ more practical.\\
